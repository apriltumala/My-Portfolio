Profile

Bridging the tactile heritage of print with the kinetic rhythm of motion.

Hello! I’m April Rose Tumala, a graphic designer and illustrator with an obsession for detail — whether that's calculating the precise fold of a packaging dieline or fine-tuning the easing curve of an After Effects keyframe.

My creative practice lives at the intersection of the physical and the digital. Because I work across both tactile and screen mediums, I’m constantly thinking about how a visual identity moves, breathes, and feels in someone’s hands.

I believe great design should be minimal without feeling sterile, and refined while staying warm and approachable. When I’m not testing paper stocks or tweaking bezier handles, you’ll find me collecting botanical lithographs, visiting print archives, or animating experimental loops.

Academic Reflections

Reflection: Professionalism in IT

When I first began studying Information Technology, I thought professionalism was mostly about looking put-together for a job interview, a neat resume, a firm handshake, the right vocabulary during a presentation. As I've progressed through my coursework, group projects, and early exposure to real IT environments, I've come to understand that professionalism in this field runs much deeper than appearances. It is a mindset that touches how I write code, how I communicate with teammates, and how I take responsibility for my own growth.

One of the clearest lessons has come from working on group projects. In IT, no meaningful system is built by one person alone, and I've learned that professionalism shows up in the small, unglamorous habits: showing up to meetings prepared, documenting my code so others can understand it, and communicating honestly when I'm stuck instead of letting a deadline quietly slip. Early on, I underestimated how much trust depends on these habits. A missed commit, an undocumented function, or a vague status update can derail a whole team's momentum. I've realized that professionalism isn't a performance for supervisors, it's a form of respect for the people who depend on my work.

Ethics has also become a central part of how I think about professionalism. IT students are taught early about data privacy, intellectual property, and cybersecurity, but it's one thing to memorize these concepts for an exam and another to internalize them as principles I'll actually live by. Handling a classmate's personal data during a systems project, or thinking about how an application I build might be misused, has made me appreciate that professionalism in IT means holding myself accountable even when no one is watching. The field moves fast, and it's tempting to cut corners, skip testing, reuse insecure code, ignore proper attribution, but professionalism means resisting those shortcuts because the consequences of carelessness in IT can be significant, from data breaches to systems failures that affect real people.

I've also learned that professionalism requires humility. Technology changes constantly, and no amount of classroom learning fully prepares a student for the pace of real-world tools and practices. I used to feel embarrassed asking questions in class, worried it would make me look unprepared. Now I see that a professional in IT is not someone who knows everything, but someone who is honest about what they don't know and is committed to continuously learning through documentation, mentorship, and simply being willing to be a beginner again and again as technology evolves.

Finally, professionalism has shaped how I present myself, both online and offline. As someone building a portfolio and preparing for internships, I've learned that professionalism extends to how I communicate in emails, how I give and receive feedback, and how I carry myself in interviews and networking events.

Ultimately, professionalism in IT, for me, is not a checklist but a discipline, one built on accountability, ethics, collaboration, and a genuine commitment to growth. As I move closer to entering the workforce, I hope to carry these lessons beyond the classroom and into every system I help build.

Reflection: Introduction to AI Literacy and Responsible Use

Going into this topic, I assumed I already understood artificial intelligence fairly well. As an IT student, I had used AI tools for coding help, research, and even brainstorming design ideas for my portfolio work. But "Introduction to AI Literacy and Responsible Use" showed me that knowing how to use AI tools is very different from understanding them, how they work, where their limitations lie, and what responsibilities come with relying on them.

The first thing that reshaped my thinking was learning how AI models are actually built and trained. I had a vague notion that AI "learns from data," but I hadn't fully grasped how much the quality, scope, and biases of that training data shape everything the model produces. Realizing that an AI system can sound confident while being completely wrong, a phenomenon I learned to recognize as "hallucination", changed how I approach using these tools. I used to accept AI-generated answers at face value, especially when they were phrased fluently. Now I treat AI output the way I'd treat a helpful but occasionally unreliable classmate: useful for a first draft or a starting point, but never a final authority without verification.

This course also pushed me to think critically about ethics in ways I hadn't before. Discussions on data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the environmental cost of training large models made me realize that "responsible use" isn't just about avoiding academic dishonesty, though that's part of it. It's about understanding the broader footprint of the tools I use daily. Learning about how biased training data can lead to biased outputs, sometimes with real consequences for marginalized groups, made me more conscious of questioning results rather than passing them along uncritically, especially in a field like IT where the systems we build can scale those biases quickly.

As a student, the most practically useful part of the course was learning where the line is between using AI as a support tool and letting it replace my own thinking. I had to be honest with myself about moments when I used AI to shortcut effort rather than to genuinely learn, asking it to solve a problem outright instead of using it to understand the concept behind the problem. This reflection was uncomfortable at times, but necessary. Professionalism and academic integrity, I realized, both depend on being transparent about how much of a piece of work is genuinely mine versus AI-assisted, and using disclosure honestly rather than avoiding the question.

The course also gave me language and frameworks I didn't have before, concepts like prompt literacy, human-in-the-loop review, and the importance of verifying AI outputs against credible sources. These aren't just academic ideas, they're skills I now see as essential for any IT professional, since AI tools are already embedded in software development, design, and everyday workflows.

Looking back, this experience didn't just teach me how to use AI more effectively, it taught me to use it more thoughtfully. As I continue building my skills and my portfolio, I want to carry this literacy forward: staying curious about how these tools work, honest about their limits, and responsible about the role they play in my growth as a future IT professional.
